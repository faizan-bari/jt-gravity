Step 4 of 5

Two sides, one wormhole

The two boundaries of the black hole are joined through the interior by a wormhole. Its length is the one thing pure JT gravity can do: it shrinks, bounces and grows, like a particle thrown at a wall.

Measuring the wormhole

Pick a time on each boundary, \(t_L\) and \(t_R\). The spacelike geodesic between the two boundary points crosses the wormhole. Its length \(d\) diverges as \(\varepsilon\to0\), because both boundaries are infinitely far away, so the divergence has to be subtracted. In Harlow and Jafferis’s normalisation (see the table on the conventions page), the renormalised length is

\[ \tilde L=d-2\ln\left(2\Phi_\partial\right),\qquad \Phi_\partial=\frac{\phi_b}{\varepsilon},\qquad \phi_b=\frac{\phi_r}{16\pi G}. \tag{4.1} \]

The result, derived below, is

\[ \tilde L=2\ln\left[\frac{\cosh\big(\pi(t_L+t_R)/\beta\big)}{\Phi_h}\right],\qquad \Phi_h=\frac{2\pi\phi_b}{\beta}=\frac{\phi_h}{16\pi G}. \tag{4.2} \]

Only \(t_L+t_R\) matters

The length depends on the two times only through their sum. That comes from a symmetry: the boost that leaves the dilaton (2.3) unchanged moves the right boundary forward in time and the left one backward. In pure JT the two boundary energies are always equal, \(H_L=H_R\) (Harlow and Jafferis (2.20)), so evolving with \(H_R-H_L\) does nothing to the state. Press Boost: the geodesic slides along, and its length stays fixed.

It grows forever

At late times \(\cosh x\approx e^x/2\), so

\[ \tilde L\approx\frac{2\pi}{\beta}\,(t_L+t_R)-2\ln\left(2\Phi_h\right). \tag{4.3} \]

The wormhole grows linearly, at a rate set by the temperature, and it is shortest on the time-symmetric slice \(t_L+t_R=0\). This is the two-dimensional version of the growth of the Einstein–Rosen bridge behind the horizon (Hartman and Maldacena). Classically the growth never stops. At times of order \(e^{S_0}\), effects beyond the semiclassical approximation stop it (Iliesiu, Mezei and Sárosi).

A particle bouncing off a wall

Harlow and Jafferis show that pure JT gravity with two boundaries has a two-dimensional phase space, with \(\tilde L\) and

\[ P=2\Phi_h\tanh\frac{\pi(t_L+t_R)}{\beta} \tag{4.4} \]

as canonically conjugate variables. The total energy \(H=H_L+H_R\) becomes

\[ H=\frac{P^2}{2\phi_b}+\frac{2}{\phi_b}\,e^{-\tilde L}. \tag{4.5} \]

This is a non-relativistic particle of mass \(\phi_b\) in an exponential potential. Run the clocks forward and the “particle” comes in from \(\tilde L=\infty\), turns round at the wall where \(2e^{-\tilde L}/\phi_b=H\), and goes back out. The wormhole was long in the past (the white-hole era), is shortest at \(t_L+t_R=0\), and grows again. It is exactly solvable quantum mechanics as well: the energy eigenfunctions are modified Bessel functions of \(e^{-\tilde L/2}\) (Harlow and Jafferis §3.1), and that is how JT gravity with two boundaries is quantised.

Derivation of (4.2), and a check of (4.4)–(4.5)

Set \(\ell=1\). A boundary point at time \(t\) sits where (C.7) equals \(\phi_r/\varepsilon\), that is \(\cos\sigma=\varepsilon\,(2\pi/\beta)\cos\tau\). Write (2.7) as \(\cos\tau=1/\cosh a\), \(\sin\tau=\tanh a\) with \(a=2\pi t/\beta\). Then, to leading order in \(\varepsilon\), the embedding coordinates (C.6) of the point are

\[ T_1=\frac{\beta}{2\pi\varepsilon},\qquad T_2=\frac{\beta}{2\pi\varepsilon}\sinh a,\qquad X=\pm\frac{\beta}{2\pi\varepsilon}\cosh a, \tag{4.6} \]

with \(+\) on the right boundary and \(-\) on the left. On the hyperboloid the geodesic distance between two points is fixed by their inner product, \(\cosh d=-X_L\cdot X_R=T_1T_1'+T_2T_2'-XX'\), so

\[ \begin{aligned}\cosh d&=\left(\frac{\beta}{2\pi\varepsilon}\right)^2\left(1+\sinh a_L\sinh a_R+\cosh a_L\cosh a_R\right)\\ &=2\left(\frac{\beta}{2\pi\varepsilon}\right)^2\cosh^2\frac{a_L+a_R}{2}.\end{aligned} \tag{4.7} \]

Since \(d\) is large, \(d=\ln(2\cosh d)=2\ln(\beta/\pi\varepsilon)+2\ln\cosh\big(\pi(t_L+t_R)/\beta\big)\). Subtracting \(2\ln(2\phi_b/\varepsilon)\) and using \(\Phi_h=2\pi\phi_b/\beta\) gives (4.2). It matches Harlow and Jafferis (2.32) with \(\delta=(t_L+t_R)/2\).

For (4.4)–(4.5), take \(\delta=(t_L+t_R)/2\) as the time that \(H\) generates, since \(H=H_L+H_R\) moves both clocks forward together. Differentiating (4.2) gives \(d\tilde L/d\delta=P/\phi_b\) and \(dP/d\delta=(2/\phi_b)\,e^{-\tilde L}=-\partial H/\partial\tilde L\), which are Hamilton’s equations for (4.5). Substituting (4.2) and (4.4) gives \(H=2\Phi_h^2/\phi_b\), which is twice the one-sided energy \(E\) of (C.10). The figure checks this numerically as you move the sliders.

Why matter changes everything

In pure JT, \(H_L=H_R\) always. So the two-sided Hilbert space does not split into a left part and a right part; Harlow and Jafferis call this the factorisation problem. Matter changes that. The pulse of step 3 is the simplest state in which the two boundaries have different energies: afterwards the right boundary has \(E+E_s\) and the left still has \(E\). With quantum matter fields, Penington and Witten show that the observables of one boundary, its Hamiltonian together with matter operators dressed to that boundary, form a Type II\(_\infty\) von Neumann algebra. That algebra has a trace, and therefore a well-defined entropy.
