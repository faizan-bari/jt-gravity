Step 3 of 5

Throw something in

Throw a pulse of matter into the black hole. The geometry stays exactly AdS₂ and only the dilaton changes. Yet the horizon moves outward, and it starts moving before the pulse is even thrown.

A shock wave

The simplest thing to throw in is a thin pulse moving at the speed of light, a shock wave. It leaves the right boundary at boundary time tst_sts​ carrying energy EsE_sEs​, measured by the boundary clock. In null coordinates x±=τ±σx^\pm=\tau\pm\sigmax±=τ±σ the metric (1.1) is ds2=−e2ωdx+dx−ds^2=-e^{2\omega}dx^+dx^-ds2=−e2ωdx+dx− with e2ω=1/cos⁡2σe^{2\omega}=1/\cos^2\sigmae2ω=1/cos2σ (ℓ=1)(\ell=1)(ℓ=1), and the pulse travels along the ingoing light ray x+=xs≡τs+π/2x^+=x_s\equiv\tau_s+\pi/2x+=xs​≡τs​+π/2, where τs\tau_sτs​ is the global time at which it leaves the boundary, from (2.7). Its stress tensor is T++=p δ(x+−xs)T_{++}=p\,\delta(x^+-x_s)T++​=pδ(x+−xs​), with ppp constant along the pulse.

In these coordinates g++=0g_{++}=0g++​=0, so the ++++++ component of (2.2) keeps only its derivatives:

e2ω ∂+ ⁣(e−2ω ∂+ϕ)=−8πG T++.(3.1)e^{2\omega}\,\partial_+\!\left(e^{-2\omega}\,\partial_+\phi\right)=-8\pi G\,T_{++}. \tag{3.1}e2ω∂+​(e−2ω∂+​ϕ)=−8πGT++​.(3.1)

Integrating across the pulse, ϕ\phiϕ is continuous and its derivative along x+x^+x+ jumps:

Δ(∂+ϕ)=−8πG p.(3.2)\Delta\left(\partial_+\phi\right)=-8\pi G\,p. \tag{3.2}Δ(∂+​ϕ)=−8πGp.(3.2)

The matter does not bend the geometry; it bends the dilaton. Positive energy makes ϕ\phiϕ fall off faster beyond the pulse, the focusing of (C.4a).

The solution

Before the pulse (x+<xs)(x^+<x_s)(x+<xs​) nothing has changed and ϕ\phiϕ is (2.3). After it, add a vacuum solution that vanishes on the pulse, so that ϕ\phiϕ stays continuous:

The horizon moves, early

The right event horizon is the past light cone of the boundary’s last moment:

x−=τ−σ=τend−π2.(3.9)x^-=\tau-\sigma=\tau_{\rm end}-\frac{\pi}{2}. \tag{3.9}x−=τ−σ=τend​−2π​.(3.9)

With positive energy it moves outward, toward the boundary. Because it is fixed by the boundary’s entire future, it moves along its whole length, including long before the pulse was thrown. The chart under the controls shows ϕ\phiϕ along the event horizon. It starts at ϕh\phi_hϕh​, grows like e2π(t−ts)/βe^{2\pi(t-t_s)/\beta}e2π(t−ts​)/β as the throw approaches, and after the pulse has crossed it stays at ϕh′\phi_h'ϕh′​, the value (3.5). The horizon grows before anything has fallen in.

Nothing travels backwards in time here. The event horizon is defined by the whole future, so it “knows” what is coming; local quantities near it do not change until the pulse arrives. The same thing happens in four dimensions when a shell falls into a black hole. With positive energy, ϕ\phiϕ on the event horizon never decreases, the two-dimensional area theorem.

Negative energy: a traversable wormhole

Classical matter cannot carry negative energy, but quantum fields can, briefly. Gao, Jafferis and Wall showed that coupling the two boundaries sends a pulse of negative null energy into the bulk. In (3.5) that means Es<0E_s<0Es​<0 and λ>0\lambda>0λ>0. Now fff stays positive longer, τend>π/2\tau_{\rm end}>\pi/2τend​>π/2, and the horizon (3.9) moves inward. Light sent from the left boundary at global time τL\tau_LτL​ travels along x−=τL+π/2x^-=\tau_L+\pi/2x−=τL​+π/2, and it reaches the right boundary if

τL<τend−π.(3.10)\tau_L<\tau_{\rm end}-\pi. \tag{3.10}τL​<τend​−π.(3.10)

The left boundary exists only for τL>−π/2\tau_L>-\pi/2τL​>−π/2, so a window opens exactly when τend>π/2\tau_{\rm end}>\pi/2τend​>π/2: signals sent from the left early enough come out on the right, after the pulse. Set a negative energy in the figure to see them.

Two caveats. In the real effect the negative energy is small and quantum; the slider goes to Es=−0.8EE_s=-0.8EEs​=−0.8E only to make the window visible. And the signals’ own backreaction is ignored. Maldacena, Stanford and Yang work out the JT version in detail.

Checks on (3.3)–(3.10)

All of these were checked symbolically or numerically before publishing. On the pulse, the added piece in (3.3) vanishes and its ∂+\partial_+∂+​ derivative equals λ\lambdaλ. It satisfies ∇μ∇νϕ=gμνϕ\nabla_\mu\nabla_\nu\phi=g_{\mu\nu}\phi∇μ​∇ν​ϕ=gμν​ϕ (ℓ=1)(\ell=1)(ℓ=1). Where the new horizon (3.9) meets the pulse, the old dilaton already equals ϕh′\phi_h'ϕh′​, so ϕ\phiϕ is continuous there and constant along the horizon afterwards. For small EsE_sEs​, (3.6) agrees with βEs\beta E_sβEs​ to second order. The late-time slope ∣f′(τend)∣|f'(\tau_{\rm end})|∣f′(τend​)∣ equals ϕh′\phi_h'ϕh′​ from (3.4) to six decimal places.

What this does not show

There are no quantum fields here, so the black hole does not radiate, and AdS still reflects everything at its edges. Step 5 opens the box.
