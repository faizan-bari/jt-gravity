Where is the black hole?

In JT gravity the geometry is always the same AdS₂, so there is no way to drop a black hole into it. The black hole is written in a second field, the dilaton, and in where the boundary sits.

Gravity without curvature

JT gravity has a metric gμνg_{\mu\nu}gμν​ and a scalar ϕ\phiϕ, the dilaton. The action is eq. (C.1) on the conventions page. In the charged black hole we zoomed into, ϕ0+ϕ\phi_0+\phiϕ0​+ϕ measures the area of the sphere at each point of the throat: ϕ0\phi_0ϕ0​ is its value at extremality and ϕ≪ϕ0\phi\ll\phi_0ϕ≪ϕ0​ is the small excess. The equations of motion are

R=−2ℓ2,(2.1)R=-\frac{2}{\ell^2}, \tag{2.1}R=−ℓ22​,(2.1) ∇μ∇νϕ−gμν∇2ϕ+1ℓ2 gμν ϕ=−8πG Tμν.(2.2)\nabla_\mu\nabla_\nu\phi-g_{\mu\nu}\nabla^2\phi+\frac{1}{\ell^2}\,g_{\mu\nu}\,\phi=-8\pi G\,T_{\mu\nu}. \tag{2.2}∇μ​∇ν​ϕ−gμν​∇2ϕ+ℓ21​gμν​ϕ=−8πGTμν​.(2.2)

The first comes from varying ϕ\phiϕ. It fixes the metric to be AdS₂ everywhere, whatever the matter is doing, as long as the matter does not couple to ϕ\phiϕ directly (the standard choice). All of gravity’s response to matter sits in (2.2), which is an equation for ϕ\phiϕ. There are no gravitational waves in two dimensions, and in JT there is not even any curvature that can change.

In vacuum, (2.2) reduces to ∇μ∇νϕ=gμνϕ/ℓ2\nabla_\mu\nabla_\nu\phi=g_{\mu\nu}\phi/\ell^2∇μ​∇ν​ϕ=gμν​ϕ/ℓ2. Its solutions are the linear functions of the embedding coordinates (C.6), and the one describing a black hole with two boundaries is

ϕ=ϕh cos⁡τcos⁡σ.(2.3)\phi=\phi_h\,\frac{\cos\tau}{\cos\sigma}. \tag{2.3}ϕ=ϕh​cosσcosτ​.(2.3)

The others are this one moved by a symmetry of AdS₂, or turn negative at one of the edges, which the boundary conditions rule out (Harlow and Jafferis, §2.1).

The boundary

The dilaton is large near the edges, where the throat opens out into the rest of the black hole. JT gravity puts its boundary on the curve where

ϕ∣bdy=ϕrε,γtt=−1ε2,ε→0,(2.4)\phi\big|_{\text{bdy}}=\frac{\phi_r}{\varepsilon},\qquad \gamma_{tt}=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^2},\qquad \varepsilon\to0, \tag{2.4}ϕ​bdy​=εϕr​​,γtt​=−ε21​,ε→0,(2.4)

where γtt\gamma_{tt}γtt​ is the induced metric, which defines the boundary time ttt. As ε→0\varepsilon\to0ε→0 the curve hugs the edge, but only while −π/2<τ<π/2-\pi/2<\tau<\pi/2−π/2<τ<π/2. Outside that range cos⁡τ<0\cos\tau<0cosτ<0, so ϕ\phiϕ is negative near the edge and can never reach ϕr/ε\phi_r/\varepsilonϕr​/ε. The boundary’s entire history, ttt from −∞-\infty−∞ to ∞\infty∞, fits between τ=−π/2\tau=-\pi/2τ=−π/2 and τ=π/2\tau=\pi/2τ=π/2.

The horizon

The right boundary ends at the corner (σ,τ)=(π/2,π/2)(\sigma,\tau)=(\pi/2,\pi/2)(σ,τ)=(π/2,π/2). The last light ray to reach it travels along σ=τ\sigma=\tauσ=τ, and nothing above that line can ever send a signal to the right boundary, so σ=τ\sigma=\tauσ=τ is its future event horizon. The lines σ=±τ\sigma=\pm\tauσ=±τ cut the strip into four regions: the two exteriors, the black hole interior above and the white hole interior below. They cross at the centre, the bifurcation point.

On these lines cos⁡τ=cos⁡σ\cos\tau=\cos\sigmacosτ=cosσ, so ϕ=ϕh\phi=\phi_hϕ=ϕh​ all along them: the horizon’s area is constant, as it should be for a black hole in equilibrium. Look at the lines of constant ϕ\phiϕ. Outside the horizons they are timelike, so with a rocket you can hover at a fixed sphere size. Inside they are spacelike, and ϕ\phiϕ decreases toward the future whatever you do, just as rrr does inside a Schwarzschild horizon.

The dashed lines where ϕ=−ϕh\phi=-\phi_hϕ=−ϕh​ are where the charged black hole has its inner horizon. Beyond them the solution is not determined by data at the two boundaries, so the diagram stops there (Harlow and Jafferis, Fig. 2).

Falling in

Release a particle from rest on the τ=0\tau=0τ=0 slice at σ1\sigma_1σ1​. By (1.3) it follows

sin⁡σ=sin⁡σ1cos⁡τ,(2.9)\sin\sigma=\sin\sigma_1\cos\tau, \tag{2.9}sinσ=sinσ1​cosτ,(2.9)

and crosses the horizon σ=τ\sigma=\tauσ=τ where tan⁡τ×=sin⁡σ1\tan\tau_\times=\sin\sigma_1tanτ×​=sinσ1​. Its proper time is finite there. From (1.5), with e=1/cos⁡σ1e=1/\cos\sigma_1e=1/cosσ1​, ds=ℓcos⁡σ1 dτ/(1−sin⁡2σ1cos⁡2τ)ds=\ell\cos\sigma_1\,d\tau/(1-\sin^2\sigma_1\cos^2\tau)ds=ℓcosσ1​dτ/(1−sin2σ1​cos2τ), which integrates to

As it falls, the particle sends a light signal to the right boundary every s×/6s_\times/6s×​/6 of its own time. A signal sent from (σe,τe)(\sigma_e,\tau_e)(σe​,τe​) reaches the boundary at global time τe+π/2−σe\tau_e+\pi/2-\sigma_eτe​+π/2−σe​, and (2.7) turns that into boundary time. The signals arrive further and further apart. The one sent from the horizon arrives at t=∞t=\inftyt=∞, and signals sent from inside never arrive.

Close to the horizon the delay grows logarithmically: each time the particle halves its remaining time to the crossing, the arrival time at the boundary grows by (β/2π)ln⁡2(\beta/2\pi)\ln 2(β/2π)ln2. This exponential stretching between the infaller’s clock and the boundary’s clock is the core of the standard argument that Hawking radiation is thermal at temperature 1/β1/\beta1/β.

What comes next

Nothing here has any matter in it. In step 3 we throw something in. The metric will stay AdS₂, but the dilaton will change, the boundary curve will bend, and the horizon will jump outward.
